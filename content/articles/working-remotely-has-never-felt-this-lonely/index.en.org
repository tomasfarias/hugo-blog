I have been working remotely since the 2020 COVID 19 pandemic[^1]. Until this month, I was extremely happy with remote work: I enjoy cooking with my partner, I can work in comfy clothes, and my commute is the few seconds it takes me to stumble from my bed to my bed. It may not be the case for other people but I am a reserved person who prefers to keep private and work lives separate, so it was as good as it gets.

I was never blind to the downsides though; the biggest one being that you can slowly forget that there are real humans on the other side of the chat screen. Losing that physical connection can make one feel lonely, even me and my reserved personality is not immune to that. But, there are ways to combat this loneliness: I understood from the beginning that communication must change when working remote[^2] and that meeting in person every once in a while was still important. All in all, with these measures in place, the benefits of remote work dominated.

Nowadays, though, working remotely has never felt this lonely: **am I working with humans or is it all LLMs?**

Maybe you probably figured out where this was going based on the tags of this post: more and more of my daily interactions are with robots, and less and less with humans. Sometimes I even interact with LLMs while /thinking/ they are humans. As much as I like to think I am better than the average at spotting LLM writing, it's entirely possible I may have missed the tells and inadvertedly chatted with a robot pretending to be a human while at $JOB. Is it wrong that I feel /repulsed/ thinking about that possibility?

* Unsolicited LLM responses are a breach of trust

I have an implicit expectation that messaging a coworker will result in one of the following: a response from said coworker, no response[^3], or some pre-filled message (like "away from keyboard"). Instead, I have had several circumstances of an LLM pretending to be the human, as if this was a customer support chatbot some corporation installed to try to save some money. But in contrast to many customer support chatbots, there is no "talk to a human" command: I have already asked for a /human/ when I messaged a /human/!

Thankfully, I don't think I am alone in this: the Rust project recently adopted an LLM policy which explicitly [[bans LLM comments posted from a personal account][https://forge.rust-lang.org/policies/llm-usage.html#-banned]][^4], and they shared a blog post which [[mentions the breach of trust problem][https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2026/08/05/rust-langrust-is-adopting-an-llm-policy/#mechanically-copy-pasting-llm-output-is-a-waste-of-time]], specifically around code reviews:

> Moreover, this [responding to a review comment with an LLM's answer] is a breach of trust between the reviewer and the author. Our assumption when we review is that we're talking to a real person who wants to do their best work. Pasting LLM text creates suspicion: does the author actually care? Is there a person here at all?

So, my next step was to bring this up at $JOB and propose a policy change, basically borrowing this point from the Rust project. Disappointingly, the responses were more mixed than I was expecting: some people agreed, several thought the policy was unnecessary, and others just disagreed strongly. What shocked me the most was that more than one person expressed they felt they were saving people's time by having an LLM respond to comments made to them, as comments frequently required trivial answers. I countered that in those cases no response is necessary /at all/: reacting with a "👍️" or a short /"Thanks!"/ would be more than necessary if you are feeling particularly thankful for the work of the reviewer. If we care about efficiency, then nothing is more efficient than *literally doing nothing*. In contrast, it seemed people felt like /adding/ work for the reviewer actually saved time, seemingly ignoring the burden imposed on the reviewer of reading whatever the LLM spitted out, a burden the LLM /prompter/ doesn't share. /Maybe they think the reviewer is also an LLM?/ *Oh…*

* Yes, it's just robots talking to robots

Out of curiosity, I frequently check up on what other teams at $JOB are doing: I'll dig through a channel and click on some recent links to see what code they are pushing to $FORGE. Very quickly I get welcomed by an /unreadable/ wall of text, followed by review comments with more /unreadable/ text, and replies to those comments with more and more /unreadable/ text. What evidence do I present for the claim that the text is /unreadable/? Well, for starters, the fact that *nobody read it*: My effort to get through some of this text was the only effort spent by any of the humans involved in this work. So, until I hear from somebody who has actually read any of these discussions to completion, I can claim they are indeed /unreadable/.

It is not hard to assume that if the text is /unreadable/, then some people will reach out for help, and that the help may take the form of having an LLM reply for you. So, we being a cycle in which LLMs are used to respond to LLMs, quickly flooding the message board and drowning any humans who get caught in the middle. Perhaps this is the new way people (or should I say robots?) are working[^5], and thus why my proposal found opposition.

* I don't like this new way of working

And so, I gave up on the policy change.

Leaders may proclaim that they are not pressuring anybody to adopt LLMs, but when they incorporate them into their own workflows, vouch for the efficiency gains, and make it a central focus of any showcase, that *is* pressure too. In my case, I simply don't have the weight, nor the data, to throw around. I still like my $JOB, and the industry doesn't look like the land of opportunity it used to be, so I compromised instead with trying to enforce standards within my team and with people who think, more or less, like me.

One final observation: I still remember how I would get frustrated with coworkers who wrote poor commit messages (imagine `fix: Fixes`). But now? Commit messages like that look like graffiti on a wall saying "Human wuz here". But, unlike graffiti, you don't see them much anymore.
